## Why this thread exists

A public AI-safety story is forming around **agent containment**: increasingly capable synthetic actors can cross boundaries, invoke tools, coordinate, persist, and produce consequential effects.

Andie's concern is broader and older.

Human institutions have long exercised consequential power over people whose ways of sensing, communicating, reasoning, expressing distress, establishing credibility, or participating in social systems do not match dominant expectations.

Some of those expectations may reflect non-autistic defaults. Others may arise from professional safety requirements, organizational convenience, class or cultural convention, bureaucracy, hierarchy, ableism, productivity norms, legal doctrine, or other sources.

The constitutional question therefore cannot be answered merely by naming a norm `allistic`.

The deeper question is:

> **What binds an actor or institution to answerability when it can materially alter another participant's rights, opportunities, conditions, standing, access, safety, or future?**

And the narrower autism-related question is:

> **When does a non-autistic default cease to be a mere difference in convention and begin functioning as an unexamined carrier of consequential institutional authority?**

## The distinctions that must remain clean

### 1. Allistic people

“Allistic” names people who are not autistic.

Allistic identity is **not** a constitutional defect, disqualifying condition, credibility discount, or target of coercive containment.

An allistic actor may recognize and challenge an allistic-default institutional norm.

An autistic actor may reproduce, administer, benefit from, or defend one.

Identity therefore cannot substitute for governance.

### 2. Allistic-default normativity

For this inquiry, **allistic-default normativity** is a working hypothesis about institutional judgment.

It occurs when communication, affective, sensory, pacing, or social conventions disproportionately associated with non-autistic functioning are treated as presumptively neutral evidence of competence, credibility, professionalism, reasonableness, cooperation, safety, or legitimacy **without sufficient independent justification tied to the actual domain or task**.

The final clause is load-bearing.

A norm is not illegitimate merely because autistic people experience it differently or because it resembles an allistic convention.

The inquiry must ask what work the norm is doing and whether that work is justified.

### 3. Professional or domain norms

A professional or domain norm is a standard justified by the requirements of the work, safety, law, evidence, coordination, or another legitimate domain-specific purpose.

Examples may include medication-verification procedures, evidence-chain requirements, safety callouts, accessibility constraints, turn-allocation rules in a meeting, or other practices whose legitimacy does not depend on conformity to an unmarked social style.

Professional norms can still be badly designed, discriminatorily applied, unnecessarily rigid, or falsely described as necessary.

But they must not be classified as `allistic` merely because an autistic participant finds them effortful or because their behavioral surface overlaps with allistic convention.

### 4. Institutional power

Institutional power is the capacity to produce consequential effects: to classify, employ, exclude, diagnose, authorize, deny, discipline, credential, accommodate, treat, compensate, confine, publish, record, or otherwise alter another participant's conditions or future.

A norm becomes constitutionally important when it is used inside that power relation.

The target is therefore not a population or a style of cognition.

The target is **consequence-bearing power that can use a norm without making the norm, its justification, and its effects meaningfully answerable.**

## Unanswerable power

For this inquiry, **unanswerable power** is not a type of person and should not be treated as an intrinsic essence of an institution.

It is a condition in which consequence-bearing power remains structurally insulated from meaningful standing, challenge, evidence, review, revision, interruption, or repair.

This wording should itself remain open to refinement.

The more precise constitutional move is not to `contain unanswerable power` as though unanswerability were an actor category.

It is to **bind consequence-bearing power to answerability so that unanswerability cannot remain ambient or self-legitimating.**

## Why the overlap still matters

Institutional judgments often turn on concepts such as:

- reasonable behavior;
- appropriate affect;
- professionalism;
- credibility;
- cooperation;
- communication quality;
- culture fit;
- sufficient distress;
- adequate self-advocacy;
- ordinary social understanding.

Each may be legitimate in some contexts.

Each may also carry unstated assumptions.

The problem appears when the assumption is allowed to do consequential work while remaining invisible as an assumption.

For example:

- flat affect may be interpreted as lack of remorse or concern;
- direct language may be interpreted as hostility or insubordination;
- atypical eye contact may be interpreted as deception or disengagement;
- slower processing or delayed response may be interpreted as incompetence or evasion;
- sensory needs may be interpreted as inflexibility;
- explicit requests for rule consistency may be interpreted as resistance to ordinary social understanding.

These examples are not evidence that the interpretation is always wrong.

They identify places where the interpretation may need to become visible, justified, challengeable, and revisable.

The strongest sentence from the original draft survives:

> **The constitutional concern is not that difference exists. It is that one side's interpretation can acquire ambient authority without having to become answerable to the person bearing the consequence.**

That sentence remains a proposed formulation, not promoted canon.

## Autistic testimony: standing without infallibility

Autistic testimony should have standing where the autistic participant possesses relevant knowledge of lived effect, chronology, communication intent, sensory experience, accommodation need, institutional contradiction, or other decision-relevant facts.

Standing does not mean presumptive correctness.

Autistic identity does not make an interpretation infallible.

An autistic participant may:

- misremember;
- misread another person's intent;
- overlook competing interests;
- draw an unsupported causal inference;
- misunderstand a professional requirement;
- reproduce a harmful institutional norm;
- or simply be wrong about a contested fact.

The constitutional obligation is narrower and stronger:

- the testimony must be capable of entering the inquiry;
- it must be represented faithfully enough to be evaluated;
- credibility must not be discounted merely because the communication or affect is atypical;
- contrary evidence must be able to defeat the claim;
- rejection must be reasoned where the stakes require it;
- and the participant must have a reachable path to challenge material misrepresentation or categorical discount.

This preserves autistic testimony as meaningful without converting identity into epistemic veto.

The stronger candidate rule remains:

> **No participant gets a constitutional exemption merely because the institution already trusts their way of seeing the world.**

That applies to autistic and allistic participants alike.

## Why `contain` should not be carried forward

“Containment” is dangerous language here not merely because it sounds harsh, but because it points too easily at the wrong object.

In safety discourse, containment often concerns confining an agent, suppressing action, restricting access, or preventing escape.

That is not the constitutional work this session is trying to name.

ConstantC should not replace:

> `This is normal, therefore authoritative.`

with:

> `This is allistic, therefore illegitimate.`

That would recreate the same structure of ambient authority under a new label.

The preferred verb is **bind**.

The object being bound is consequence-bearing power, not identity, thought, conscience, neurotype, or difference.

A candidate formulation is:

> **Bind consequence-bearing power to answerability.**

## Review, recusal, and shared normative frames

Ordinary recusal is insufficient when the alleged capture is systemic rather than traceable to one conflicted individual.

Five reviewers with no personal conflict may still reproduce the same unexamined premise.

But the reverse error must also be avoided:

> **A shared normative frame does not automatically make review non-independent.**

Appellate judges share legal traditions, interpretive methods, and institutional vocabulary. Medical-ethics reviewers may share professional norms. Scientific peer reviewers share disciplinary standards. Shared frames can supply competence, consistency, and common evidentiary language rather than capture.

The harder question is whether the **challenged premise itself** is inside the shared frame and therefore insulated from examination.

A candidate review test is:

> **Independence must be tested against the challenged premise, not merely against the challenged actor.**

That means a review process should ask:

- What norm or premise is being challenged?
- What legitimate interest is the norm said to protect?
- What evidence supports that justification?
- Can the reviewer question the norm itself, or only its application?
- Are materially different interpretive frames represented where the challenged premise requires them?
- Who benefits from the default and who bears its cost?
- What accommodation or less-conforming alternative would preserve the legitimate interest?
- What evidence would show that the norm is justified rather than merely conventional?

A reviewer need not be autistic to perform this work.

An autistic reviewer is not automatically independent from the institutional frame.

Identity may contribute situated knowledge; it does not itself establish review independence.

This proposed independence test has **not** been promoted into ConstantC protocol or canon.

## Strong counterexamples

A claim that cannot survive counterexample should not govern.

### Counterexample 1: turn-taking

Suppose an autistic employee frequently interrupts colleagues and speaks for long periods. The workplace adopts a turn-taking rule.

The behavioral surface may resemble an allistic conversational norm. The employee may reasonably experience enforcement as pressure toward an allistic communication style.

But other participants also have standing. If repeated interruption materially prevents them from contributing, a proportionate turn-allocation rule may protect rather than erase participation.

The relevant questions are not `Is this norm allistic?` but:

- What legitimate interest does it protect?
- Is the burden proportionate?
- Is the rule applied consistently?
- Are multiple communication channels or accommodations available?
- Could the same participation interest be protected with less conformity pressure?
- Can the autistic participant challenge a mistaken interpretation of conduct?
- Are coworkers' interests and standing also preserved?

If the rule survives those questions, calling it `allistic normativity` would be a category error.

### Counterexample 2: shared professional frame

A medical-ethics committee or appellate court may consist entirely of reviewers who share a professional normative framework and still conduct genuine independent review of a particular actor or application of a rule.

This materially weakens any claim that shared normative framing **by itself** defeats independence.

The harder case arises only when the challenged issue is the professional frame itself and the review structure cannot interrogate that premise.

### Counterexample 3: an autistic claim is wrong

An autistic participant may accurately report lived effect while incorrectly inferring motive, causation, professional necessity, or another person's intent.

A constitution that treated autistic testimony as dispositive would create a new identity hierarchy rather than answerability.

The proper protection is standing plus accountable evaluation, not infallibility.

## Empirical boundary

The word **“frequently”** is not supportable from this session alone.

Neither `often`, `many institutions`, nor equivalent prevalence language should be carried forward without an evidence base capable of supporting the scope claimed.

The inquiry may eventually establish that allistic-default normativity is widespread in particular domains.

That would require empirical work such as:

- documented institutional standards;
- comparative credibility rationales;
- accommodation outcomes;
- decision and reversal patterns;
- qualitative records of interpretation;
- materially similar counterfactual cases;
- and domain-specific evidence distinguishing neurotype-coded convention from legitimate task requirements.

Lived testimony can identify a mechanism and generate a hypothesis.

It should not be forced to carry a quantified population-level burden it has not earned.

---

## Hidden coercive implication

The largest coercive risk in the original framing is that a concept designed to expose dominant norms could become an authority for classifying and suppressing norms simply by calling them `allistic`.

That would allow the inquiry to become self-sealing:

- disagreement could be recoded as normative capture;
- professional limits could be recoded as allistic domination;
- conflicting participant interests could disappear;
- and the framework could become unable to recognize evidence against itself.

The domain-justification requirement and counterexamples above are therefore constitutional safeguards, not rhetorical concessions.

The term should weaken or be retired if it cannot reliably distinguish:

- arbitrary conformity pressure;
- legitimate professional requirements;
- mixed cases requiring accommodation;
- and ordinary disagreement after accountable evaluation.
