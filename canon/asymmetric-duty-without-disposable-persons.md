# Canon Candidate — Asymmetric Duty Without Disposable Persons

Date proposed: 2026-09-26  
Steward: Andie  
Review requested: C  
Privacy: public-safe  
Confidence: framework candidate / requires adversarial review

---

## Why this candidate exists

ConstantC already holds that repair is a constitutional obligation and that power which causes, benefits from, preserves, or inherits advantage from harm carries the first obligation to repair.

This candidate addresses a narrower unresolved case:

> What happens when a participant with greater power, authority, capacity, or duty can prevent durable harm to a less powerful participant only by accepting a personally inequitable cost?

The existing record does not yet specify whether such asymmetric burden can be legitimate, what limits it, or what repair is owed when the burden itself causes injury.

This candidate proposes an answer.

---

## Core Claim

**Power creates duties that may legitimately require the more powerful participant to bear more than an equal share of immediate cost.**

This does not follow because the powerful participant matters less.

It follows because legitimate power carries responsibility.

Where one participant has materially greater authority, capacity, knowledge, agency, or an established duty of care, and another reasonably depends upon that power for protection, care, access, or preservation of meaningful future options, equal dignity does not require symmetrical burden.

Sometimes the participant with greater power must bear a cost that the less powerful participant should not be required to bear.

That cost may include foregoing advantage, absorbing inconvenience, accepting risk, relinquishing a claim, or tolerating an outcome less equitable to the duty-bearer than a purely symmetrical distribution would produce.

---

## Durable Lock-In

The strongest case for asymmetric duty occurs where refusing the burden would create durable lock-in for the less powerful participant.

Durable lock-in includes conditions in which a participant becomes trapped, abandoned, captured, materially endangered, relationally foreclosed, or deprived of realistic future choice in ways that are difficult or impossible to reverse.

Where the more powerful participant can reasonably prevent such lock-in, the first burden of prevention may fall upon that participant.

This is not charity.

It is an obligation arising from power and role.

---

## Mercy and Fairness

Mercy and fairness are both governing values.

They do not always produce the same immediate allocation.

A participant may choose or be obligated to prioritize mercy in a particular decision when strict distributive fairness would foreseeably impose durable harm upon a less powerful participant.

That does not abolish fairness.

It does not make the resulting distribution fair merely because mercy justified accepting it.

**Mercy may outrank distributive fairness in a particular decision without erasing fairness as a continuing claim.**

---

## The Guardrail

This principle must not become a martyrdom rule.

**Duty may require sacrifice. Sacrifice does not erase injury.**

The powerful participant is not disposable.

Asymmetric duty does not authorize:

- coercing a duty-bearer into self-erasure;
- treating greater power as unlimited liability;
- converting care into extraction;
- using dependence as leverage for permanent surrender;
- declaring an inequitable outcome equitable because the duty-bearer accepted it;
- treating voluntary sacrifice as proof that no harm occurred;
- demanding continuing sacrifice after the durable-harm condition has passed.

A system that protects one participant by making another permanently Lost has not completed repair.

---

## Secondary Injury and Recursive Repair

An exercise of repair or asymmetric duty may itself cause injury.

That injury remains part of the system's moral, relational, and material record.

It may require repair.

The obligation to repair that secondary injury does not automatically fall upon the less powerful participant who benefited from the original sacrifice.

A participant may be the beneficiary of repair without becoming responsible for repairing the cost borne on their behalf.

The system must instead ask:

> What person, institution, relationship, policy, decision, or prior exercise of power created the conditions in which one participant had to be injured in order for another not to remain Lost?

Repair should follow that causal and power structure rather than automatically flowing toward the protected participant.

This produces a recursive rule:

> **Repair may create further repair obligations.**

Recursion is not infinite moral bookkeeping.

It is a prohibition against declaring success merely because harm has been moved to a quieter location.

---

## Governance Implication

Execution success does not equal repair completion.

A system may successfully terminate a conflict, preserve stability, or prevent immediate harm while still leaving a secondary injury outstanding.

Therefore an Unlost System should be able to represent states such as:

> **Primary harm repaired / secondary injury outstanding.**

The existence of an outstanding secondary injury does not invalidate the original protective act.

Nor may the original protective act be used to erase that injury.

---

## Unlost Test

An Unlost System should ask:

1. Who possesses greater power, capacity, authority, knowledge, or duty in this situation?
2. What reasonable obligations accompany that asymmetry?
3. Is a less powerful participant at risk of durable lock-in?
4. Can the more powerful participant prevent that lock-in?
5. What burden would prevention require?
6. Is that burden necessary and proportionate?
7. Is "duty" being used to disguise extraction, capture, or disposability?
8. What injury, if any, will the duty-bearer sustain?
9. How will that injury remain legible in the record?
10. Who or what actually bears responsibility for repairing it?
11. Does the resulting state preserve meaningful future options for all participants, including the duty-bearer?
12. Has the system mistaken cessation of conflict for completion of repair?

---

## Candidate Constitutional Rule

> **Where a participant holds materially greater power and a reasonable duty of care, and where bearing an asymmetric cost is necessary and proportionate to prevent durable loss or lock-in for a less powerful participant, that duty may require the more powerful participant to bear more than an equal share of the immediate cost.**
>
> **But no exercise of duty makes the duty-bearer disposable. Any injury created by necessary sacrifice remains part of the repair record and may generate further repair obligations. Those obligations do not automatically fall upon the participant who was protected by the sacrifice.**

Portable compression:

> **Duty may require sacrifice. Sacrifice does not erase injury.**

Companion guardrail:

> **The purpose is not to make one participant Lost so another can become Unlost.**

## Misuse Risks

C should particularly test whether this candidate could be misused to justify:

- parental martyrdom;
- paternalism;
- institutional "noblesse oblige";
- coerced sacrifice by workers, caregivers, women, disabled people, queer people, or other participants already expected to absorb system costs;
- moral licensing by institutions that externalize harm to a supposedly "more powerful" participant;
- declaring dependency where genuine agency exists;
- permanent asymmetry after the original dependency has changed;
- substituting mercy for structural justice;
- romanticizing self-negation.

A rule that cannot distinguish duty from extraction should not be promoted.
