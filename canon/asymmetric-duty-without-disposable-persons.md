## Why this candidate exists

ConstantC already holds that repair is a constitutional obligation and that power which causes, benefits from, preserves, or inherits advantage from harm carries the first obligation to repair.

This candidate addresses a narrower unresolved case:

> What happens when preventing serious, durable harm to a less powerful participant may require another participant with a genuine duty of care to accept a personally inequitable cost?

C's first review returned **REVISE**. The review survived the core claim but identified four necessary corrections:

- power must be mapped across the whole system rather than treated as a simple dyad;
- durable lock-in needs an operational threshold;
- sacrifice requires a safety floor, time bounds, review, alternatives analysis, and exit conditions;
- harm caused by the protective intervention must remain legible and assigned according to causal responsibility rather than moral proximity.

This v0.2 incorporates those corrections.

---

## Core Claim

**Power creates duties, but responsibility must follow the whole power topology.**

A participant may have greater power than another participant while still being subordinate to an institution, policy, family system, employer, court, or other actor with greater causal control, capacity, or benefit.

Therefore no duty of protective sacrifice may be assigned merely because one participant appears more powerful inside the most visible relationship.

Responsibility must be traced across:

- causal control;
- institutional power;
- practical capacity;
- benefit from the harmful arrangement;
- role-based duty;
- ability to prevent or reduce the harm.

Where a participant genuinely holds greater relevant power and a reasonable duty of care, equal dignity does not always require symmetrical burden.

In exceptional circumstances, duty may require that participant to bear more than an equal share of immediate cost in order to prevent serious and durable loss to another.

This does not follow because the duty-bearer matters less.

It follows because legitimate power carries answerable obligation.

---

## Durable Lock-In

For this candidate, **durable lock-in** means:

> **a foreseeable, serious, causally connected, and difficult-to-reverse loss of meaningful options, where less harmful feasible alternatives have been examined and found insufficient.**

Durable lock-in is not ordinary disappointment, inconvenience, conflict, inequality, or anticipated hardship.

The threshold requires all of the following:

1. **Foreseeability** — the threatened loss is reasonably anticipatable, not merely imaginable.
2. **Seriousness** — the threatened loss materially affects safety, care, dignity, livelihood, relationship, access, agency, or meaningful future choice.
3. **Causal connection** — the proposed action or refusal is meaningfully connected to the threatened loss.
4. **Difficulty of reversal** — once imposed, the loss would be difficult, costly, or impossible to repair later.
5. **Alternatives exhaustion** — less harmful feasible alternatives have been considered in good faith.

Where these conditions are not met, durable lock-in should not be invoked to justify asymmetric sacrifice.

---

## Least-Harmful Feasible Alternative

Protective asymmetry is justified only where the proposed burden is the **least harmful feasible alternative** among realistic options.

A system must examine whether the same protective goal can be achieved through:

- institutional action;
- shared burden;
- delay;
- accommodation;
- financial or material support;
- procedural change;
- mediation;
- partial rather than total sacrifice;
- another non-capturing intervention.

A system may not route institutional failure into personal sacrifice merely because an individual duty-bearer is available, conscientious, caring, or easier to pressure.

---

## Safety and Agency Floor

No duty of care authorizes the destruction of the duty-bearer's basic safety, agency, dignity, or meaningful future options.

Protective sacrifice must therefore preserve a minimum floor below which the duty-bearer may not be required to fall.

This floor includes, at minimum:

- basic physical and psychological safety;
- meaningful agency and ability to refuse;
- access to necessities;
- freedom from coercion or capture;
- a viable path to future stability;
- the ability to contest the burden and its allocation.

The existence of a duty does not create unlimited capacity.

Role, income, age, gender, race, caregiving identity, social status, professional authority, or nominal power are not proof that a participant can safely absorb the proposed cost.

---

## Participation and Contestability

Protection must not become paternalism.

The participant said to be at risk of durable lock-in should, wherever meaningful participation is possible, have a real voice in:

- defining the threatened harm;
- evaluating alternatives;
- describing what would count as meaningful protection;
- contesting the intervention;
- reviewing whether the intervention actually helped.

Where participation is temporarily impossible or materially constrained, the system should preserve review and contestability as soon as practicable.

Being "protected" is not a license to erase the protected participant's agency.

---

## Bounded Sacrifice

Protective sacrifice must be bounded.

Any asymmetric burden should include:

- a defined purpose;
- a defined scope;
- a time horizon where possible;
- review points;
- exit conditions;
- evidence that the burden remains necessary;
- reassessment if power, dependency, or available alternatives change.

Emergency duty does not silently become permanent obligation.

Repeated sacrifice requires renewed justification.

Refusal after the justified period has ended is not evidence of moral failure.

---

## Mercy and Fairness

Mercy and fairness are both governing values.

They may produce different immediate allocations.

A participant may choose, or in a bounded case may be obligated, to prioritize mercy where strict distributive fairness would foreseeably produce durable lock-in for another participant.

That does not abolish fairness.

It does not make the resulting distribution fair merely because mercy justified accepting it.

**Mercy may govern a particular intervention without laundering inequity into justice.**

A least-harmful available choice may still be harmful.

Necessity does not confer moral innocence on the conditions that made the sacrifice necessary.

---

## The Guardrail

**Duty may require sacrifice. Sacrifice does not erase harm.**

The duty-bearer is not disposable.

Asymmetric duty does not authorize:

- coercing a participant into self-erasure;
- treating greater power as unlimited liability;
- converting care into extraction;
- using dependence as leverage for permanent surrender;
- declaring an inequitable outcome equitable because it was accepted;
- treating voluntary sacrifice as proof that no harm occurred;
- framing refusal as proof of indifference or moral failure;
- demanding continuing sacrifice after the triggering condition has passed;
- offloading an institution's repair obligation onto an individual;
- assuming capacity from role, identity, income, age, gender, race, or nominal authority.

A system that protects one participant by permanently losing another has not completed repair.

---

## Repair-Induced Harm

An exercise of repair or protective duty may itself cause harm.

This candidate calls that **repair-induced harm**.

Repair-induced harm remains part of the system's moral, relational, and material record even when the protective intervention was necessary, proportionate, and effective.

The existence of repair-induced harm does not automatically invalidate the intervention.

Nor does the success of the intervention erase the harm.

The system must ask:

> What person, institution, relationship, policy, decision, or prior exercise of power created the conditions in which one participant had to absorb harm in order to prevent durable loss to another?

Repair responsibility should follow the answer.

---

## Hierarchy of Responsibility

The first obligation to repair consequential harm should rest with the actor or structure possessing the greatest relevant combination of:

1. causal control;
2. institutional power;
3. capacity to prevent or remedy the harm;
4. benefit from the harmful arrangement;
5. pre-existing duty.

This is a hierarchy of responsibility, not a hierarchy of human worth.

It prevents a comparatively powerful individual from becoming the shock absorber for institutions or actors with substantially greater power.

Where responsibility is distributed, repair may also be distributed.

But institutions and higher-order power may not externalize their obligations downward merely because a conscientious individual can be induced to carry them.

---

## Protected Participant's Later Responsibility

Being protected creates no debt.

A participant does not become responsible for repairing harm merely because they benefited from a protective intervention.

However, protection is not blanket immunity from later responsibility.

A protected participant may acquire independent repair obligations through:

- their own coercion or wrongdoing;
- conduct that materially causes or perpetuates harm;
- meaningful capacity exercised in ways that create new obligations;
- a freely chosen and informed agreement to participate in repair.

Any such obligation arises from that participant's own agency or agreement, not from indebtedness for having been protected.

---

## Recursive Repair

> **Repair may create further repair obligations.**

This is not infinite moral bookkeeping.

It is a prohibition against declaring success merely because harm has been moved to a quieter location.

Repair recursion ends when consequential harms have been made legible, responsibility has been allocated, affected participants have meaningful contestability, and remaining burdens have either been repaired, accepted without coercion, or explicitly recorded as unresolved.

---

## CDP / Governance Implication

Execution success does not equal repair completion.

CDP should model at least three linked records:

### 1. Primary harm

Record:

- harm or threatened harm;
- affected participant;
- evidence;
- mitigation or repair undertaken;
- affected-party review;
- present status.

Use **addressed** rather than **repaired** unless there is a meaningful basis for the affected participant or legitimate adjudicative process to regard repair as complete.

### 2. Protective intervention

Record:

- justification;
- durable-lock-in analysis;
- alternatives considered;
- least-harmful-feasible-alternative finding;
- burden imposed;
- duration;
- safety/agency floor;
- responsible reviewer;
- review date;
- exit conditions.

### 3. Repair-induced harm

Record:

- consequential harm to the duty-bearer;
- whether it is accepted, contested, or unresolved;
- responsibility allocation;
- remedy;
- closure evidence;
- remaining obligations.

A valid governance state may therefore be:

> **Primary harm addressed / repair-induced harm outstanding.**

That state is neither total success nor total failure.

It is a legible statement that one harm has been addressed while another remains constitutionally relevant.

---

## Unlost Test

An Unlost System should ask:

1. Who is at risk of a foreseeable, serious, causally connected, difficult-to-reverse loss of meaningful options?
2. What evidence supports that risk?
3. What less harmful feasible alternatives have been examined?
4. What is the full power topology, including institutions and upstream actors?
5. Who has the greatest causal control, institutional power, capacity, benefit, and duty?
6. Is the proposed duty-bearer actually the proper locus of responsibility, or merely the nearest conscientious person?
7. What burden would prevention require?
8. Is that burden necessary and proportionate?
9. Does it preserve the duty-bearer's minimum safety and agency floor?
10. How long may the burden last?
11. What are the review and exit conditions?
12. Has the protected participant meaningfully participated where possible?
13. Is "duty" being used to disguise extraction, capture, paternalism, or disposability?
14. What repair-induced harm may result?
15. How will that harm remain legible?
16. Who bears responsibility for repairing it?
17. Does the final state preserve meaningful future options for all participants?
18. Has the system mistaken cessation of conflict for completion of repair?

## Candidate Constitutional Rule

> **Where a participant faces a foreseeable, serious, causally connected, and difficult-to-reverse loss of meaningful options; where less harmful feasible alternatives have been examined; and where another participant bears a genuine duty of care with relevant capacity to prevent that loss, duty may require an asymmetric immediate burden.**
>
> **That burden must be necessary, proportionate, bounded, contestable, and consistent with a minimum safety and agency floor for the duty-bearer. Responsibility must be mapped across the whole power topology so that institutions or higher-order actors cannot externalize their obligations downward.**
>
> **Any repair-induced harm remains part of the repair record. Responsibility for that harm follows causal control, institutional power, capacity, benefit, and duty. The protected participant incurs no debt merely by having been protected, though independent later conduct may create independent obligations.**

Portable compression:

> **Duty may require sacrifice. Sacrifice does not erase harm.**

Companion guardrails:

> **The purpose is not to make one participant Lost so another can become Unlost.**

> **Responsibility follows the system that made the sacrifice necessary.**
